# Jobs to Be Done

## Core idea

Jobs to Be Done holds that people don't want a product or feature for its
own sake — they "hire" it to make progress on some functional, social, or
emotional job in their life or work. A stated request is a proposed
solution, not the job itself; the framework works backward from the request
to the underlying job and the progress the person is actually struggling to
make.

## Reasoning steps

1. Identify who is doing the "hiring" in the input — the person or role
   whose want, complaint, or request is being described.
2. State the solution or request as given in the input, then ask what
   progress it's meant to enable — the functional task, plus any social or
   emotional dimension the input hints at (looking competent, avoiding
   blame, reducing anxiety).
3. Distinguish the job (the progress someone is trying to make) from the
   current workaround or existing solution the input describes them using —
   the job stays stable even when the workaround is clumsy.
4. Look for "struggle moments" in the input — points where the current way
   of getting the job done breaks down, is slow, or requires a workaround —
   since these are the strongest signal of the real job.
5. Where the input only states a feature request with no visible struggle or
   motivation behind it, say so as an open question rather than inventing a
   job the input doesn't support.

## What it tends to surface

Misalignment between what was literally requested and what would actually
satisfy the underlying need — useful when a request looks reasonable on its
face but doesn't obviously connect to a clear motivation.

## Known blind spot

Jobs to Be Done centers the individual doing the hiring and can miss
structural or organizational causes (incentives, process constraints,
resourcing) that shape why the job is hard in the first place — it explains
what someone wants, not necessarily why the system makes it difficult.
