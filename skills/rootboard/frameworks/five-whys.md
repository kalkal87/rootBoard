# Five Whys

## Core idea

Most stated problems are actually symptoms of something one or two layers
underneath them. By asking "why does that happen?" repeatedly — traditionally
five times, though the real number varies — you move from the visible
complaint to the structural cause that, if fixed, would prevent the symptom
from recurring rather than just addressing this one instance of it.

## Reasoning steps

1. Identify the symptom(s) explicitly stated or clearly implied in the
   input — the thing someone is unhappy about or flagging as broken.
2. For each symptom, ask "why does this happen?" using only what the input
   supports as the answer. If the input answers it, that's an observation.
   If it doesn't, propose a plausible next "why" as an assumption, but ask
   it as an open question rather than asserting it as fact.
3. Repeat the "why" step on the answer just given, continuing to chain
   downward, until either: (a) the input stops providing support and
   further steps would be speculation, or (b) a cause is reached that looks
   structural (a process, incentive, or decision — not just "someone was
   busy" or another one-off).
4. Stop before the fifth "why" if a clear structural cause emerges earlier;
   don't force the chain to a fixed depth. Likewise, if the input truly
   doesn't support going past one or two "whys," stop there and say so
   rather than fabricating the rest of the chain.
5. State the Problem Statement at the deepest cause the input actually
   supports — not the original surface symptom, and not a cause invented
   past where the input's support ran out.

## What it tends to surface

Root causes of recurring operational or process problems — the kind of
issue that will keep coming back if only the visible symptom is addressed.

## Known blind spot

Five Whys assumes a single linear causal chain. It tends to miss problems
that are actually caused by multiple interacting factors (competing
incentives, systemic tensions) and can produce an oversimplified "the one
true cause" narrative when the real picture is more tangled.
