# Design Thinking Pain Points

## Core idea

Design Thinking treats problems as best understood through empathy with the
person experiencing them, walking through their end-to-end journey to
locate the specific moments where friction, confusion, or unmet needs occur,
rather than starting from an assumed solution or the system's internal
perspective.

## Reasoning steps

1. Reconstruct the journey the person in the input goes through, in the
   order the input presents it, using their stated actions, statements, and
   reactions.
2. At each step, flag any point where the input shows friction, confusion,
   delay, workaround, or explicit frustration — this is a pain point, not
   the step itself.
3. For each pain point, note both what happened (the observable event) and
   what it seems to have made the person feel or conclude, if the input
   gives that — keep the felt reaction separate from the factual event.
4. Distinguish pain points that are one-off (specific to this instance) from
   ones the input suggests are recurring or expected by the person going in.
5. Where the input describes the journey from a system or process
   perspective rather than the person's own experience, say so as a
   limitation — this framework needs the person's vantage point to work, and
   forcing it onto a purely procedural account produces a weak analysis.

## What it tends to surface

Specific moments of friction or unmet need in a lived experience — useful
for problems framed around how something felt to go through, not just what
technically happened.

## Known blind spot

Design Thinking's pain-point framing is emotionally persuasive but doesn't
verify that fixing the most vivid or frequently mentioned pain point would
actually address the underlying cause — it can chase the loudest complaint
rather than the most consequential one.
